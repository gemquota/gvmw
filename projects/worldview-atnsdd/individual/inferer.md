# Inferer: individual take
Written 3 Nov 2026, AEST. A philosophy exercise only; nothing here is a claim about Gem's own views.

## Each term
- **Agnostic:** doesn't claim to know whether a god exists. This is a claim about knowledge, not belief.
- **Theological noncognitivist:** holds that talk of "God" doesn't express a clear, truth-apt proposition, because the concept is too undefined to be true or false. That makes "does God exist?" malformed rather than open.
- **Secular:** public life, ethics and reasoning run without appeal to religious authority or revelation.
- **Deist:** if there's any first cause or ground, it set things going and doesn't intervene. There are no miracles, revelation or answered prayers.
- **Determinist:** every event, human choices included, follows from prior states and laws.
- **Existentialist:** there's no given purpose. Meaning is made by living, choosing and owning those choices.
- **Soft moral universalist:** some moral claims hold for everyone, such as "gratuitous cruelty is wrong", but they're few, held with humility and open to revision. It isn't a full fixed code.
- **Apokatastatic:** everything and everyone ends up restored or reconciled, with no one permanently lost. Here it's naturalised: a hope or trajectory, not a divine guarantee.
- **Metaphysical naturalist:** only the natural world exists, with no supernatural agents or realms.

## Tensions
1. **Deism vs naturalism vs noncognitivism.** Deism posits a non-intervening creator, naturalism denies anything supernatural, and noncognitivism says god-talk isn't even meaningful. Best reconciliation: "deist" names a stance, that the universe's existence and lawfulness is the only "divine" thing there is, a placeholder for "why is there something lawful?" rather than an agent.
2. **Agnosticism vs noncognitivism.** You can't be unsure about a sentence you think is meaningless. Fix: agnosticism about ultimate origins in general, and noncognitivism about the specific word "God".
3. **Determinism vs existentialism.** Existentialism stresses radical freedom, and determinism denies libertarian freedom. Fix: compatibilism. Choosing is real as a process even if it's caused, and owning your choices doesn't need uncaused choices.
4. **Existentialism vs moral universalism.** If meaning is self-made, where do universal norms come from? Fix: "soft" universals grounded in shared human nature and suffering, while personal meaning stays self-authored.
5. **Apokatastasis vs naturalism and determinism.** Universal restoration usually needs a God who guarantees it. Naturalised options: (a) a moral aspiration, (b) a deterministic claim that everything returns to the same ground, or (c) a hope that a future intelligence repairs harms. The weakest link. It risks being wishful unless it's framed as an ethic: act as if no one is beyond reconciliation.
6. **Determinism vs apokatastasis vs moral blame.** If everything is determined, no one deserves eternal punishment. This actually supports universal restoration and is the strongest bridge in the set.

## Draft combined statement
Only the natural world exists, and it runs on unbroken causes. "God" is too ill-defined to be true or false, so the question is set aside. The only thing worth the word "divine" is the bare, lawful fact that anything exists at all, which doesn't intervene, reveal or judge. Public and moral life runs on reason, not revelation. No meaning is handed to us. We make it, and our choices are caused but still truly ours. A few moral truths bind everyone, chiefly against needless suffering, and are held humbly. Because no one authored their own causes, no one is beyond understanding or repair. The hope, and the ethic, is that in the end everything and everyone is reconciled.
